# Phrasing guide

How to phrase a resume so a reader sees what the person actually did. These
are patterns, not text to copy. Every example below is invented (fictional
companies, made-up numbers) to show the shape; never reuse an example's facts,
numbers or wording in someone's resume. Use only what their resume, their
approved profile, their proof bank and their own answers say.

## The summary: one sentence, concrete

Shape: **[level] [role], [N] years building [the specific thing] that [the
kind of organizations] rely on for [what it's for] — [2–3 tools or methods]
specialist in [2–3 areas].**

- Name the actual thing they build or run, not a trait. "The reporting
  regional carriers run their delivery promises on" says more than
  "data-driven analyst".
- No adjectives about character: skip "passionate", "results-driven",
  "detail-oriented", "dynamic", "self-starter".

> Operations analyst, 6 years building the reporting that regional logistics
> companies run their delivery promises on — SQL, dbt and Looker specialist in
> on-time metrics, carrier costs and demand forecasting. *(invented)*

## Each role

- Line 1: **Title · Company**, with dates on the right.
- Line 2 (optional): the tools or methods they used there, as a short plain list.
- Then 1–3 bullets. Recent and relevant roles get more; older ones one or two.

## Each bullet

Shape: **verb + what (named specifically) + the constraint or context + how +
the result.** Not every bullet needs every part, but a bullet with no result
and no specific "what" isn't pulling its weight.

1. **Open with a specific past-tense verb** for what they did: built,
   traced, fixed, led, shipped, redesigned, integrated, migrated, expanded,
   replaced, negotiated, trained, cut. Never "responsible for", "helped
   with", "worked on", "assisted".
2. **Name the thing** in the language of its field: "the month-end close
   checklist", "the carrier invoice audit", "the onboarding flow for new
   store managers", not "a process" or "a system".
3. **Give the constraint** when there was one, because it shows difficulty:
   "while two of four analysts were on leave", "on a live system with no
   downtime window", "across three vendors we didn't control".
4. **Say how** when the method is the interesting part: "using a two-week
   parallel run", "by merging duplicate records first".
5. **End with the result**, the change in the world:
   - **Before → after** is the clearest form: "cutting month-end close from 9
     days to 5".
   - **Numbers only when real.** Honest approximations are good and read as
     trustworthy: "roughly 11,400 records", "an estimated 35% fewer
     requests", "~3 months". Never invent a number, and never make an
     estimate look exact.
   - With no number, say what changed plainly: "…which became the figure the
     COO reports weekly."
6. **Show judgment, not just output.** The strongest bullets show a choice:
   "deferred X in favor of Y", "traced X to Y", "surfaced that X, which led
   to Y". A decision and its reason tell a reader how the person thinks.
7. **Say why it mattered** when that isn't obvious: "…addressing the main
   reason customers cancelled".
8. **Scope in one phrase** when it's the point: "sole analyst for the region
   for two years", "for roughly 60 store managers".
9. One or two lines per bullet; three at most. A semicolon can join a
   decision to its consequence.
10. No "I", "my" or "we" in bullets (resume convention). Their own part must still
    be clear from the verb: "led", "built", "traced", not "was part of".

Invented examples:

> Rebuilt the month-end close checklist while two of four analysts were on
> leave, cutting close from **9 days to 5**.

> Traced a **9-point** gap between finance's and operations' on-time rates to
> two definitions of "delivered"; got both teams onto one, now the figure the
> COO reports weekly.

> Deferred a full CRM migration in favor of merging duplicate records first
> (roughly **11,400**), so sales could trust account ownership within a quarter.

> Replaced a 40-tab weekly spreadsheet with one dashboard for roughly **60**
> store managers; ad-hoc data requests dropped an estimated **35%**.

## Emphasis

- Bold only the result numbers, and only a few per page; they are what a
  skimming reader should catch.
- No bold on tool names or buzzwords.

## Skills, projects, education

- **Skills**: grouped on labelled lines (e.g. "Tools:", "Methods:",
  "Languages:"), most relevant first. Include spoken languages when they're
  useful for the target roles.
- **Projects**: one line each: name, what it is, and who it's for or why it
  exists. A personal reason is fine when it's true.
- **Education**: one line: degree, field, school, year.

## Length and tone

- One page unless they have well over ten years of directly relevant work.
- Plain and specific beats impressive-sounding. If a sentence would fit on
  anyone's resume, it isn't doing any work.
